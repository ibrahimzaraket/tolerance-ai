Methods for Estimating
Forecast Turnover

Exercise 1 — Identify and evaluate the different adapted methods to estimate the forecast turnover of a project, and precise the modalities of the choices made.

Section 1

Offer Approach

Supply-side methods estimate turnover by benchmarking against existing competitor data and sector statistics, anchoring projections in observable market reality.

1a
Competitive / "Analog" Method

Estimation of the potential commercial turnover from competitor figures published by competitors or obtained through direct questioning of companies. Relies on analogy with established players operating in the same segment.

Source — Commercial Court Registry (CR & balance sheet), société.com, direct company inquiry Limit — For a brand-new activity, no reliable reference figure may exist
1b
"Reference" / Accounting Sector Statistics Method

Estimation of the potential turnover based on accounting sector statistics, taking into account the economic environment of the future company, the range of products sold, the target clientele, limits to the activity, and the resources implemented (hours or days billed).

Source — Approved management centers, professional organisations, sector ratio databases
Section 2

Demand-Driven Approach

Demand-side methods estimate turnover from the customer perspective — through field surveys and market-share modelling. They tend to produce higher (optimistic) figures and must be treated with caution.

2a
"Purchase Intentions" Method

Estimation of the potential turnover based on data collected in the field survey: quantities usually purchased, frequency of purchase, psychological price, average basket, etc. Aggregates stated willingness to pay across the identified target population.

Source — Field survey / primary quantitative research Limit — Significant discrepancy between expressed intentions and actual purchases; generally a very high WTP assumption — apply a conservative correction (–30 to –50 %)
2b
"Targets and Market Share" Method

Estimation of potential turnover by applying the company's desired or anticipated market share to the total addressable market. Depends on the volume of unconquered potential customers ("relative non-consumers") and the level of competitive intensity.

Source — Market study, quantitative inquiry, industry market-size reports Limit — Based on assumed demand, take-up and penetration rates; consider with great caution
Section 3

Alternative Approaches

These methods do not rely primarily on competitor benchmarks or customer surveys, but instead set viability thresholds or derive projections from real operational experience.

3a
"Break-Even Point" Method

Estimate of the potential turnover at which the business would make neither profit nor loss — the level that just covers all fixed and variable costs. Serves as the minimum viability threshold rather than a market forecast.

Source — Approved management centers, professional organisations, BM & projected resource plan Limit — Sets a target floor, but does not predict the turnover that will actually be achieved in the market
3b
Testing & Experimentation Method

Estimate of the potential turnover based on real-life operational situations: on a test market, online, within a supporting structure (salary portage, business project support contract) or even as a micro-entrepreneur. Provides observed rather than projected data.

Source — Test market, online channel, supporting structure / business incubator Limit — Difficult to extrapolate results obtained under test conditions to full-scale autonomous activity
Section 4

Synthesis

  • These approaches are complementary — no single method is sufficient on its own.
  • Used together, they allow for the construction of the greatest possible number of scenarios, covering pessimistic, realistic, and optimistic outlooks.
  • Supply-based estimation < Demand-based estimation: offer-approach methods generally produce lower figures than demand-driven ones (maximum high hypothesis). The gap between the two should be examined critically.
  • Combining multiple methods cross-validates projections and reduces the risk of systematic overestimation or underestimation.
Section 5

Modalities of Choice

The following reasoning justifies which methods were prioritised and how they were combined to produce a credible range of turnover scenarios for the project.

  1. Anchor with the Offer Approach Start with competitor and sector-statistics data (société.com, Commercial Court Registry, approved management centres) to establish a grounded, verifiable baseline. Supply-side figures are directly comparable to existing market reality and impose a realistic ceiling before demand data is introduced.
  2. Cross-validate with the Demand Approach Conduct a field survey to gather purchase intentions and model a target market-share scenario. A correction factor of 30–50 % must be applied to stated WTP data, as the literature consistently shows that declared purchase intentions overstate actual spend.
  3. Define the viability floor with the Break-Even Method Calculate the minimum turnover required to cover all fixed and variable costs. This figure defines the survival threshold and ensures that even the pessimistic scenario sits above the point of insolvency.
  4. Validate operationally through testing where possible A pilot run — online channel, test market, or micro-entrepreneur status — provides real revenue observations before full commitment. Results are extrapolated with caution given the difference between test-market and full-activity conditions.
  5. Construct three scenarios from the combined outputs Pessimistic (offer-based floor, break-even verification), Realistic (offer + discounted demand average), and Optimistic (target market-share ceiling). This range communicates financial risk and resilience to investors, partners, and lending institutions.
Key principle — No single method yields the definitive forecast turnover. The credibility of a business plan rests on the convergence of several complementary methods: the closer the independent estimates align, the more defensible the projected figure becomes in the eyes of evaluators and financers.
